# Lesson 6 — The kick and the bend budget

Time: 45 minutes. Group: one kick demo, then paper. Benders can go back on the rack halfway through.

Students leave able to place a single small bend, and able to add a run's degrees and decide whether it needs another pull point.

## Before they come in

Codebook open to Article 358. A second bookmark in Chapter 9 at Table 2. The bench card's budget table copied onto the board so you are not flipping paper while they add. One straight stick. A square.

Read 358.24, 358.26, 358.28, and 358.30 before class, in the book this program adopted. If a distance or a subsection letter in this script disagrees with that book, teach the book and correct the script after class. The notes below are the familiar ones: Mike Holt's May 25, 2023 EC&M answer states 358.26 as a 360° limit between pull points, offsets included. Support spacing and the reaming sentence have been stable for years and still get read aloud from the open book.

## Open

A kick is one bend under 90. One mark. No flip. You use it to bring a stub over to a box that sits off the line, or to lift a run off a surface when a second bend would be more pipe than the situation needs.

The geometry is the offset geometry with the second bend missing. The end rises by the amount you choose, and it also moves along the run. For a small kick the class bends at 10° or 30° and sights it. You are not chasing a 1/16 in. landing on a kick in week one. You are chasing one plane, a readable angle, and a pipe that still looks round.

A box offset is a kick's cousin: a very small two-bend offset, usually 10°, whose depth is the standoff of the knockout. You measure that standoff from the connector and the box. You do not memorize a magic 1/4 in. This lab has no box of knockouts scheduled. The box offset waits until those parts are on the bench. The degree count does not wait. A 10° box offset spends 20°.

## Demo

Mark a stick 18 in. from the end. Arrow on the mark. Pull to 30° and stop. Lay the long leg on the bench. The short end has risen. Measure the rise so they see a single bend produce a height. Sight down the stick. One bend can still be twisted relative to the leg you meant; the hook has to start square.

That piece is the sample. Leave it.

## The budget

Open the book to 358.26 and read the sentence. The version you are confirming says there shall not be more than the equivalent of four quarter bends, 360°, between pull points such as conduit bodies and boxes. Then tell them what the sentence means on the pipe they have been bending.

Every bend between two pull points adds. A 90 adds 90. A 30° offset adds 60. A three-point saddle adds 90. A four-point saddle at 30° adds 120. A factory elbow adds the same as a bend you made. A box or a conduit body resets the count to zero. There is no maximum distance between pull points in that section. Distance is a design choice. The degrees are the limit.

358.24 is the other sentence to read. The bend is made so the tube is not damaged and the inside diameter is not effectively reduced, and the radius stays at or above Chapter 9, Table 2. Turn to Table 2 and show them the row for the size they have been bending. The shoe exists to keep them on the legal side of that table. A kink is how a student leaves it. Matching the shoe to the pipe, and keeping the foot on the pedal, is the whole of 358.24 for this course.

358.28 is why the reamer is on the bench now. Read the trimming sentence. A cut end gets reamed before a coupling or a connector goes on. Burrs cut the conductor on the pull. Have one student ream one scrap end while the others watch, even if nobody couples anything today.

358.30 is supporting. Read the fastening distances from the book, including whatever this cycle says about within 3 ft of a box and every 10 ft, and then read the exceptions yourself and leave them for a later class. Week one does not teach exceptions from memory. The pipe they just bent still has to be held up. Bending a legal shape and letting it hang is not a finished run.

## Their turn — paper

They write the total and the word pass or fail. Pull points are named in the problem. Anything between them counts.

**Run A.** From a panel to a junction box: one 90 into the room, one 30° offset, one three-point saddle, one 90 down into the box.

90 + 60 + 90 + 90 = 330. Pass. Room for a 10° kick. A second 30° offset would make 390. Fail if they add it.

**Run B.** Two 90s, one four-point saddle bent at 30°, one 10° box offset.

90 + 90 + 120 + 20 = 320. Pass.

**Run C.** Four 90s between a panel and a box, and the connector needs a 10° box offset.

360 + 20 = 380. Fail. The fix is a pull point, a conduit body or a box, somewhere in the run so each side stays at or under 360. The fix is not "the offset is small." Small offsets count.

**Run D.** Ask them to place the pull point in run C. One acceptable answer: a conduit body after the second 90. First half is 180°. Second half is 180° plus the 20° offset, 200°. Both pass. Other splits pass too, as long as each side is at or under 360 and the body is a real pull point they can open.

## Close

Walk the rack and name what they can now lay out without you: a stub from the stamp, a 30° offset, a three-point saddle, a back-to-back on the star, a four-point with the flat equal to the width.

Tell them the next time they touch a bender in this room, the shoe's stamp is still the first thing they read, and the degree total is the second. A clean saddle that puts the run at 390° is a stick you get to bend again after you add a box.
